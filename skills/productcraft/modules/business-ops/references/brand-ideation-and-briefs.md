# Brand ideation, naming and the creative brief

Read this between discovery and design: the positioning is roughly known (`brand-discovery-and-voice.md`) and the team now has to choose a strategic territory, a name, a visual mood and a story, then hand the work to designers, writers or a coding agent through a brief. Documenting a finished brand is `brand-guidelines-and-audits.md`; drawing the mark is `../../creative-media/references/logos-and-marks.md`; turning a direction into interface tokens is `../../ui-distiller/references/design-system-operations.md`.

## Ground rules

- **Diverge, then converge, at every stage.** One option is not a decision. Generate several, write down why each could win and how each could fail, then let the owners pick.
- **Every direction names what it refuses.** A territory, mood or voice without an explicit "not this" drifts until it means everything.
- **Strategy before artifacts.** No logos, palettes or taglines until the territory is chosen. Ideation output is direction, not finished assets.
- **The owners choose; you make the choice easy.** Present options side by side with the same fields filled in, so differences are visible.
- **Reference brands are vocabulary, not targets.** Saying "editorial and quiet, like a literary quarterly" helps a designer; copying a company's palette, type and layout is trade-dress risk and produces a lookalike. Write the traits you want, never the source.
- **Facts and claims come from the owners** (`../GUIDE.md`, ground rule 3). A proof point you cannot source becomes `[[NEEDS PROOF]]`.

## Stage 1: strategic territories

A territory is the ground the brand will hold that alternatives do not. Produce three to five, each from a different angle:

| Angle | The claim takes the form | Typical weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome | "The quickest, safest or cheapest way to get X done" | Competitors can match a feature in a quarter |
| Feeling | "How you feel when X is handled" | Hollow unless the product reliably delivers it |
| Belonging | "For people who see themselves as Y" | Shuts out adjacent buyers |
| Opposition | "Everything the incumbent is not" | The brand is defined by someone else's moves |
| First or only | "The one that does Z" | Must be provable today and defensible tomorrow |
| Conviction | "We believe W, so we do things this way" | Collapses the first time the company acts against it |

For each territory write four lines: the statement in one sentence, the evidence that makes it true now, what it gives up, and what would break it. Discard any territory whose evidence line is empty. Keep the best one or two; a hybrid is acceptable if one angle leads and the other supports.

## Stage 2: names

Name only after the territory is chosen, because the territory is the brief for the name.

**Generate wide.** Aim for 40 or more raw candidates across at least four construction methods: plain description, a borrowed image or metaphor, an invented word, a compound of two real words, a founder or place name, an abbreviation, a word from another language (with a native speaker's check). Long lists are cheap; the filter does the work.

**Filter hard.** A finalist passes every test below. Record pass, fail or unknown per test in a table so nobody re-litigates a name that already failed.

| Test | How to check |
|---|---|
| Say it | Read it aloud to three people who have not seen it; they spell it back correctly |
| Hear it | It survives a phone call and a voice assistant without being spelled out |
| Mean nothing bad | Searched in each target language and slang dictionary; a native speaker per key market confirms |
| Stand apart | A search for the name plus the category does not return a direct competitor |
| Can be owned | Domain options and social handles checked and recorded with the date; a trademark clearance search by counsel in each class and market before any spend |
| Stretch | Still fits if the company adds a second product line or a new market |
| Fit the territory | A stranger guesses the right territory from the name and one line of copy |

Trademark availability is a legal question: the agent can list obvious conflicts it finds, never declare a name "clear" (`../../legal-ready/GUIDE.md`). Keep the full candidate list as a "kill file" with the reason each was dropped; when a finalist fails clearance late, the next one is ready.

Present 8 to 12 finalists, each with its construction method, what it suggests, its risks and its test results.

## Stage 3: mood directions

Write two to four visual directions in words before anything is drawn. Each must be different enough that two designers given the same direction would produce related work, and given different directions would produce clearly different work.

For each direction record:

- **Three to five mood words** that exclude something ("hushed, exact, papery" excludes loud, playful and glossy).
- **Color territory** as relationships, not hex values: warm or cool, saturated or muted, light or dark ground, how many accents, how much contrast.
- **Type territory:** serif or sans for display and for text, geometric or humanist, wide or condensed, how much size contrast between levels.
- **Image territory:** photography (staged or candid, people or objects, lighting), illustration (line, flat, textured), icon style, texture or grain.
- **Composition:** dense or airy, symmetric or offset, grid-led or freeform.
- **What it rejects**, stated as visible things ("no gradients, no stock handshakes, no rounded pills").
- **Kinds of references:** "a museum catalog", "a field notebook", "a 1970s transit map". Name the category of artifact rather than a company.

"Modern and clean" is not a direction. If an owner offers only that, ask for three things they admire and one they dislike, and why.

### Using aesthetic families as a starting point

When the team needs a faster start, place each direction on five axes and pick the nearest family. Treat the family as a constraint on the design space, not a palette to paste.

| Axis | One end | Other end |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | Quiet, low saturation, one accent | Loud, saturated, several accents |
| Temperature | Cool neutrals, blue and grey cast | Warm neutrals, cream, earth and red cast |
| Making | Engineered: grids, mono details, exact edges | Crafted: texture, hand marks, irregularity |
| Time | Heritage: classical serifs, rules, ornament | Now: new grotesques, flat color, motion |
| Density | Airy: large margins, few elements | Packed: many modules, small type, data |

Rules when adapting a family: keep its structural choices (for example "single accent, paper ground, serif display"), move its adjustable values toward the audience (more density for experts, warmer accents for consumer wellness, cooler and darker for institutional trust), and if the closest competitor sits in the same family, push one axis hard to separate. If three or more axes resist the brief, the family is wrong; pick another rather than forcing it. When two families blend, write which one owns which dimension ("quiet family for type and space, engineered family for color and detail").

## Stage 4: the story

Pick a narrative shape that the facts support:

| Shape | Works when | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | A founder lived the problem and can say so specifically | The anecdote is invented or generic |
| Mission | The company makes costly choices that follow from it | The mission is a slogan with no behavior behind it |
| Discovery | There is a real insight or technique others missed | The "discovery" is common knowledge |
| Craft or heritage | There is a tradition being kept or renewed | The heritage is borrowed from someone else |
| Broken category | Customers already resent the status quo | It reads as attacking named rivals |
| Future | The product is a credible step toward a visible change | The future is vague or decades away |

For each candidate write a one-sentence summary, the first line as it would be told on an About page, the proof points (each sourced), and who the hero is: usually the customer, rarely the company.

## The creative brief

The brief is the handoff document. Two pages beats ten; a reader who sees only its first paragraph should still build the right thing.

```text
CREATIVE BRIEF  (project, version, date, owner)
1. In one paragraph   What is being made, for whom, why now, by when.
2. Audience           The primary person: situation, goal, what blocks them today,
                      where they will meet this work. Secondary only if it changes the work.
3. Outcomes           Each objective with a measurable signal and a time window
                      ("trial sign-ups: 3% of landing visitors within 30 days").
4. One message        The single sentence a visitor should leave with.
5. Voice              3-5 traits, each with its limit ("direct, never curt").
6. Look               The chosen mood direction, references by kind, and the reject list.
7. Deliverables       Specific items: "pricing page with plan table, FAQ and contact form",
                      not "website".
8. Constraints        Time, budget, stack and integrations, locked brand assets,
                      legal and accessibility level (WCAG 2.2 AA unless stated).
9. Field              3-5 things to feel near and 3-5 to feel far from, with reasons.
10. Sign-off          Who approves, on what artifact (prototype URL, PDF), by which date,
                      and what happens if approval is late.
Open questions        Each with an owner.
```

### Filling gaps without an interrogation

Pull everything already said in the conversation first. Then ask three to five questions that close the biggest gaps, grouped by topic. Useful prompts:

- Audience: "Who is the one customer you would clone? Who do you not want?"
- Outcome: "Six months after launch, which number tells you it worked?"
- Voice: "If the brand spoke at a dinner, how would people describe it the next day?"
- Look: "Show me three things you admire and one you dislike, and say why."
- Scope: "If the budget halved, what survives?"

### Push back before writing when you hear

| Input | Ask instead |
|---|---|
| "The [famous company] of our niche" | Which one or two qualities of that company, specifically? |
| "Everyone aged 18 to 65" | Which customer is worth the most, and what is their day like? |
| "Raise awareness" | Awareness of what, among whom, measured how? |
| "Modern, clean, minimal" | Three examples you like and what they share |
| Twenty pages of background | If a designer reads one paragraph, which should it be? |

## Output package

1. `brand-ideation.md`: chosen territory with its rejection line, 8-12 name finalists with test tables, two to four mood directions, the chosen story with sourced proof, open decisions.
2. `naming-candidates.md`: the full kill file.
3. `creative-brief.md`: the brief above, under about 1,500 words, with detail moved to appendices.

## Pitfalls

- One option presented as "the answer", or five options that are the same idea reworded.
- Naming before positioning, then reverse-engineering a story to fit a name someone loves.
- Declaring a name available from a domain search alone.
- Mood directions described with adjectives only, with no visible reject list.
- A brief that lists deliverables but no measurable outcome, or no named approver.
- Borrowing a company's look because the owner admires it, instead of naming the traits.

## Done when

- [ ] Three to five territories were written with evidence, cost and failure lines; the owners chose one and the reason is recorded.
- [ ] Every name finalist has a filled test table; trademark clearance is marked as counsel's step, not yours.
- [ ] Each mood direction has words, color, type, image and composition territories plus a reject list, with references named by kind.
- [ ] The story's proof points are sourced or marked `[[NEEDS PROOF]]`.
- [ ] The brief has a one-paragraph summary, measurable outcomes, specific deliverables, constraints including the accessibility level, and a named approver.
