# Genealogy and family history research

Read this when the user wants to trace an ancestor, plan family history research, cite or evaluate genealogical records, resolve conflicting records, or interpret DNA match evidence. The method is the same disciplined evidence work as any research; the sources are records about real families, so privacy and sensitivity matter.

## Plan before searching

When the user mentions an ancestor, first collect what they know, then agree on a question and a plan. Searching immediately produces a pile of same-name records and confirmation bias. If the user explicitly asks you to search right away, write a three-line plan and follow it.

**Intake:**

- Names with spelling variants, approximate dates, places (with the jurisdiction at the time: parish, county, state, country), and known relationships.
- Documents and photos already in hand, and what each says.
- Family stories, recorded as hypotheses to test, not facts.
- Online trees seen so far (leads only).
- Whether living people are involved, and what the user may and may not share.

**Research question:** one person, one fact, specific enough to answer. "Who were the parents of Johann Weber, born about 1852 in Württemberg, who emigrated to Ohio about 1880?" rather than "trace the Weber line".

## The standard of proof

Genealogists use the Genealogical Proof Standard. A conclusion is credible when the research meets all five elements:

1. Reasonably exhaustive research in the sources likely to hold the answer.
2. Complete and accurate citation of every source used.
3. Analysis and correlation of the evidence.
4. Resolution of conflicting evidence.
5. A soundly reasoned, written conclusion.

When the research falls short of the standard, say what is missing and what would close the gap; do not upgrade "likely" to "proved".

## Evaluate each record on three axes

| Axis | Categories | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Original (first recording), derivative (transcription, abstract, index, database entry), authored (a compiled family history) | A parish register page is original; an online index of it is derivative |
| Information (per fact) | Primary (informant had first-hand knowledge), secondary (second-hand), undetermined | On a death certificate, the date of death is usually primary; the birth date given by a child is secondary |
| Evidence (relative to the question) | Direct (answers it on its own), indirect (answers it only combined with other facts), negative (an absence that tells you something) | A son's absence from his father's will may be negative evidence of his earlier death |

Always go from an index or database hit to an image of the original record, and read the whole page: neighbors, witnesses and annotations are evidence too.

## Record types and what they answer

| Record | Typically answers | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Civil vital records (birth, marriage, death) | Dates, places, parents, spouses | Start dates differ widely by country and region; access to recent records is restricted |
| Church registers (baptism, marriage, burial) | The same, before civil registration | Language and script; parish boundaries changed |
| Censuses | Households, ages, birthplaces, occupations | US federal censuses are released 72 years after enumeration (1950 released in 2022); most of 1890 was destroyed; ages drift between censuses |
| Probate and wills | Family relationships, property | Lists children who were alive and in favor at the time |
| Land and tax records | Residence over time, neighbors, relationships | Strong for separating same-name men |
| Military service and pensions | Age, residence, family (pension files often rich) | Several record series per conflict |
| Immigration, naturalization, passenger lists | Origin, arrival, relatives at origin and destination | Origin town often only in later naturalization papers |
| Newspapers | Obituaries, notices, events | Derivative; verify against records |
| Court and city directories | Residence, occupation, disputes | Directories lag a year |
| Cemetery records and gravestones | Dates, family groupings | Stones can be erected decades later |
| DNA tests | Relationships among living testers and to their ancestors | Interpreted with documents, never alone |

Use locality guides (the FamilySearch Research Wiki is a good starting point) and archive catalogs to learn what survives for a place and period, and where.

## Strategies

- **Work backward** from the known, one generation at a time.
- **Build a timeline** per person from every record; gaps and impossibilities (a child born after the mother's death) show where records are confused.
- **Search the network:** family, associates and neighbors witness each other's records and move together; tracing them often breaks a brick wall.
- **Vary names and places:** phonetic indexes (Soundex for US records), abbreviations, Latin forms, transliterations, changed jurisdictions.
- **Reconstruct whole families,** not just the direct line; siblings' records often name parents.
- **Log negative searches:** what was searched, where, with which terms, and found nothing. They prevent repeating work and can be evidence.

```markdown
<one person, one fact>

<including family stories to test>

| Source to search | Why it could answer | Repository / access | Status |

## Log
| Date | Source searched | Terms and filters | Result (found / nothing) | Citation | Notes |
```

## Resolving conflicts

Put every source for the fact in a matrix: source, original or derivative, informant and their knowledge, date recorded relative to the event, value given. Weigh by quality, not by count: three censuses copying one informant's guess do not outweigh a baptism recorded days after birth. Explain why the losing sources are wrong (a later informant, a transcription error, a rounded age). If the conflict cannot be resolved, say so and keep both possibilities.

## Citations

Answer who created it, what it is, when it was created, where within it the information sits, and where the source is held (archive and call number, or website, database and image number). For online images, cite both layers: the original record and the website where you viewed it, with access date. Detailed citation models for genealogical sources are given in the field's standard style guide, *Evidence Explained*; follow it when the user wants publication quality.

Example: *Baptism of Johann Weber, 3 March 1852, Evangelische Kirche Musterdorf, Taufbuch 1840-1860, p. 112, entry 9; digital image, (website), accessed 27 October 2026.*

## Online trees, databases and terms

- Online trees and compiled genealogies are leads; check every claim against records.
- FamilySearch is free with an account; subscription sites hold many indexes and images. Their terms usually forbid scraping and bulk downloads: the user searches while logged in, and you help plan, read and analyze what they share.
- Transcriptions and indexes contain errors; cite the original when you have seen it.

## DNA evidence

- Test types: autosomal (all lines, about five to seven generations back), Y-DNA (direct paternal line), mitochondrial DNA (direct maternal line).
- Shared centimorgan ranges overlap between relationships (a half-sibling, a grandparent and an uncle share similar amounts). Use a probability tool built on published shared-cM data together with documentary evidence and the testers' trees; never conclude from a number alone.
- Consent and privacy: a DNA result reveals information about relatives who did not test. Upload or analyze someone else's DNA only with their consent. Explain the sites' policies on law-enforcement access and research use so the user can choose.
- Unexpected results (unknown parentage, adoption, donor conception) are life-changing for real people. Present them carefully and factually, suggest the user consider who is affected before sharing, and point to support resources.

## Privacy

Do not publish information about living people without their consent; most sites hide living people in trees for this reason. Handle sensitive records (adoption, institutional care, health, criminal records) with care, even for the dead, because descendants are alive. Processing data about living EU residents falls under GDPR.

## Checks (done when)

- [ ] Intake done; a specific question and a written plan exist before searching (or a short plan when the user asked to search at once).
- [ ] Every fact has a full citation; originals were viewed or their absence noted.
- [ ] Records evaluated by source, information and evidence type; conflicts resolved in a matrix or left explicitly open.
- [ ] Negative searches logged.
- [ ] Conclusion states whether it meets the proof standard and what would close remaining gaps.
- [ ] Living persons and DNA results handled with consent and care.
